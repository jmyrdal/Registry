## Six nutrient groups and energy

**Remember: Carbs, Protein, Fat — Vitamins, Minerals, Water.**

| Nutrient group | Main role | Energy |
|---|---|---:|
| Carbohydrates | Energy | **4 kcal/g** |
| Protein | Growth, repair, enzymes | **4 kcal/g** |
| Fat | Energy storage, membranes, vitamin absorption | **9 kcal/g** |
| Vitamins | Regulate body processes | **0** |
| Minerals | Structure and body functions | **0** |
| Water | Transport, temperature regulation, fluid balance | **0** |

**Memory: 4–4–9; the other three provide zero.**

Example: 10 g carbohydrate + 10 g protein + 10 g fat = **40 + 40 + 90 = 170 kcal**.

**Essential:** must obtain through diet because the body cannot make enough. **Nonessential:** body can make enough; still biologically important.

## BMI calculation

**Metric: BMI = kilograms ÷ meters²**

**U.S. units: BMI = 703 × pounds ÷ inches²**

Example: 90 kg ÷ (1.80 × 1.80) = **27.8**.

| Adult BMI | Category |
|---|---|
| Below **18.5** | Underweight |
| **18.5–<25** | Healthy-weight range |
| **25–<30** | Overweight |
| **30+** | Obesity |

**Memory: 18.5, 25, 30 — square the height.**

BMI screens weight relative to height; it does not directly measure body fat or distinguish fat from muscle.

**Slide discrepancy:** Slides call 35+ “extremely obese.” CDC uses **35–<40 class 2** and **40+ class 3/severe obesity**.

## Low versus very low food security

| Category | What changes? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Low food security** | **Quality and variety** decline; generally no substantial intake disruption | Buying cheaper, less balanced foods because money is tight |
| **Very low food security** | **Eating patterns and intake** are disrupted/reduced | Skipping meals or eating less because food money runs out |

**Memory: Low = quality; very low = quantity too.**

Food insecurity describes a **household’s access problem**. Hunger describes an **individual’s physical experience**.

## Household figures — 2024 data

| Group/measure | Figure |
|---|---:|
| All U.S. households experiencing food insecurity | **13.7%** |
| Number of food-insecure households | **18.3 million** |
| All households experiencing very low food security | **5.4%** |
| Households with children experiencing food insecurity | **18.4%** |
| Below-poverty households experiencing food insecurity | **39.4%** |

**Memory: 14 overall, 18 with kids, 39 below poverty; 5 very low.** Use exact figures when asked.

- **5.4% is already inside 13.7%**; do not add them.
- Children and poverty groups overlap; do not add their rates.
- A food-insecure household with children does not necessarily have children experiencing reduced intake.
- These percentages describe **households**, not individual adults.

## Food entrepreneurship timeline

| Period | Stage | Main advantage/example |
|---|---|---|
| **Before 1800** | Local markets | Specialization and local access: bakers, butchers |
| **1800s–early 1900s** | Industrialization | Mass production, canning, refrigeration, railroads: Heinz |
| **1920s–1970s** | Convenience and franchising | Speed, consistency, expansion: McDonald’s |
| **1980s–2000s** | Health and premium products | Differentiation and willingness to pay: Whole Foods |
| **2000s–2010s** | Digital platforms | Connecting market participants: DoorDash, Instacart |
| **2010s onward** | Sustainability and personalization | Alternative proteins, customization, waste reduction |

**Memory story: Local → factories → fast food → premium → apps → sustainable/personalized.**

Four pathways: **products, services, distribution/platforms, circular/waste reduction**.

## Too Good To Go

**Model:** Businesses sell discounted surplus food bags through a platform.

- **Business gains:** recovers revenue from surplus.
- **Customer gains:** lower-priced food; accepts uncertain contents.
- **Platform adds:** easier matching between surplus and buyers.
- **Potential environmental gain:** less edible food discarded.

**Exam trap: Surplus sold ≠ waste prevented.**

Ask: **What would have happened without the app?** Food might otherwise have been sold, donated or eaten. Discount sales might replace full-price purchases or encourage extra production.

**Memory: Bags sold are outputs; waste avoided requires a comparison.**

## Farm Bill

A broad, multiyear package covering:

- **Nutrition assistance**
- **Farm income and risk management**
- **Conservation**
- **Rural development, research, credit and trade**

**Why combine them?** Food production and food access are connected, and combining agricultural, nutrition and rural interests helps build political support.

**Memory: Help buyers, support farmers, conserve resources, develop rural areas.**

**Trap:** Not every food-assistance program is authorized through the Farm Bill.

## SNAP, WIC and other assistance

| Program | Remember it as | Provides / addresses |
|---|---|---|
| **SNAP / CalFresh** | **Grocery purchasing power** | Benefits for eligible households to buy food; addresses insufficient funds |
| **WIC** | **Targeted nutrition package** | Specified foods, education, breastfeeding support and referrals for eligible pregnant/postpartum/breastfeeding women, infants and children under five with nutritional risk |
| **School meals** | **Prepared meals at school** | Breakfast/lunch; reduces meal cost and preparation burden |
| **SUN Bucks** | **Summer grocery money** | Benefits for eligible children during summer; slides cite **$120 in California** |
| **Summer meal sites** | **Summer meals at a location** | Direct meal provision; travel and opening hours matter |
| **TEFAP** | **Food-bank distribution chain** | USDA foods/support flow through states to local distribution organizations |

**Legislation:** SNAP and TEFAP are Farm Bill nutrition programs. WIC and school meals have separate child-nutrition foundations. Permanent Summer EBT/SUN Bucks was established through the **Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023**.

Match the barrier:

- **Not enough money:** purchasing benefits.
- **Specific early-life nutrition needs:** WIC.
- **Difficulty preparing food:** accessible prepared meals.
- **No transportation:** closer sites, delivery or transportation support; benefits alone do not solve travel.

**Targeted:** concentrates resources, but screening can exclude eligible people.

**Universal:** broad access and less stigma, but funds people across incomes.

**Output:** meals served. **Outcome:** food insecurity reduced.

## New Deal price supports

**Goal:** Raise depressed farm prices and stabilize farmer income.

A **binding price support is above equilibrium**:

**Higher price → producers supply more + consumers demand less → surplus.**

Example: **Qs = 100; Qd = 60; surplus = 40.**

| Tool | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Nonrecourse loan** | Farmer can surrender commodity collateral instead of repaying |
| **Government surplus purchases** | Government buys excess output to sustain support |
| **Land set-aside** | Pays/rewards farmers to leave acreage out of production |
| **Historical export subsidy** | Encourages overseas sales, potentially pressuring world prices |
| **Decoupled payment** | Separates support from current output, weakening the direct incentive to expand |

These tools span different policy periods; decoupling is a later development, not a New Deal policy.

**Who gains/pays?** Supported farmers can gain; consumers may pay higher prices; taxpayers fund purchases/storage; competing foreign farmers can lose; extra production can increase resource use.

**Memory: Support the price, stimulate surplus.**

## Hog-farm odor case

**Problem:** Neighbors bear odor costs despite not being parties to the sale.

**Concept: Negative externality — a cost imposed on outsiders.**

**Who can act?** Farm operators and contracting companies can improve waste controls, contracts, monitoring and complaint response.

**Who pays?** Firms/growers may pay for controls; some costs may pass to buyers. Neighbors bear remaining harm.

**Memory: Profit inside the transaction; odor outside it.**

## PSSI case

**What happened:** Slides report at least **102 children, ages 13–17, in hazardous jobs at 13 meat-processing facilities** through sanitation contractor PSSI.

**Before contracting:** Verify staffing and age-check procedures, contractor history and oversight requirements.

**After contracting:** Check actual night-shift workers and records; provide safe reporting; investigate and correct violations.

**Lesson: Outsourcing work does not remove the need for oversight.**

**Memory: Before: verify the system. After: verify reality.**

## Friedman versus Freeman

| View | Managers should consider | Water-reuse example |
|---|---|---|
| **Milton Friedman — shareholders** | Owners’ interests, within applicable constraints | Saves costs, reduces risk, protects long-term value |
| **R. Edward Freeman — stakeholders** | Owners, workers, customers, suppliers and communities | Also protects community water resources and reduces harm |

**Memory: Friedman = owners; Freeman = everyone affected.**

**Exam point:** Both can favor the **same action for different reasons**. Stakeholders include shareholders.

## Philanthropy and CSR examples

| Example | Action/type | Evidence of success |
|---|---|---|
| **Ronald McDonald House** | **Philanthropy:** donations/fundraising help families stay near hospitalized children | Families helped and lodging/access burdens reduced |
| **Kroger** | **Food donation:** reported **119 million pounds** of surplus fresh food donated in 2024 | Food actually received/used and improved household access |
| **Milk with Dignity / Ben & Jerry’s** | **Purchasing standards:** premiums, worker protections, independent monitoring, reporting | Better working conditions and effective complaint resolution |
| **Frito-Lay Casa Grande** | **Operations:** recycled about **75% of process water** in cited 2012 documentation | Lower total fresh withdrawals, with costs and energy assessed |
| **General Mills** | **Farm/supply-chain practices:** coaching, plans, testing and soil-health support | Verified practices and improved soil/biodiversity outcomes |

**Memory: House = families; Kroger = food; Milk = workers; Frito = water; General Mills = soil.**

- **Philanthropy:** give resources. Customer Round-Up donations come from customers; do not attribute every dollar to the corporation.
- **Operations:** change production.
- **Purchasing:** change supplier incentives and requirements.
- **Strategic CSR:** business benefit and social/environmental benefit may align.

**Final trap:** Donations, signed standards and future targets are **not proof of improved outcomes**. Ask for a **baseline, actual results and verification**.
